# SCOUT 2026-11-09 — three new paradoxes for the stand

Written from background knowledge; URLs not re-fetched this session (MARKED: unverified live). Chosen to avoid the exclusion list and its close variants.

## 1. Knower paradox (Kaplan–Montague)

**Statement.** Treat "is known" as a predicate K of sentence-codes, with two plausible principles: (T) K(s) -> s (knowledge is factive) and (N) if s is a logical/self-evident truth then K(s) is known, i.e. K applies to T-instances (necessitation: K(T-instance)). Diagonalise: let d say "K(d) is false-known", precisely d := not K(d). Reasoning: suppose K(d). By T, d, so not K(d): contradiction; so not K(d) is provable. That proof uses only T and logic, so by N, K(not K(d)), i.e. K(d). Contradiction. It is the epistemic cousin of the liar but its trigger is factivity plus necessitation, not a truth predicate, and revenge strategies differ.

**Small formal model.** One atom d; K as a unary operator over {d, not d}. Dependencies: d := not K(d); K(x) holds iff x is in a set S of "derivable" sentences; closure rules: x in S -> x true (T); theorems of {T, diagonal} in S (N). Ask the stand: is any assignment of {true, false, gap/Z} to d, K(d), K(not K(d)) consistent with T and N? (Finite: 3 atoms, 2 closure rules.)

**Resolutions.**
- Typed / hierarchical knowledge: K_n applies only to sentences of lower level (Tarski-style; Montague).
- Restrict N: the proof of "not K(d)" is not itself known or knowable (Anderson, Kripke-style refusal of unrestricted necessitation).
- Treat K as an operator, not a predicate: no diagonal sentence exists (Thomason, Halbach).
- Paraconsistent / gappy: d is a truth-value glut or gap, and K tolerates it (Priest, Field-style fixed points).

**Sources.**
- SEP, Epistemic Paradoxes: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemic-paradoxes/
- Kaplan & Montague, "A Paradox Regained", Notre Dame J. Formal Logic 1 (1960) 79-90.
- Halbach, *Axiomatic Theories of Truth* (CUP), chapter on modal/epistemic predicates; also SEP, Self-Reference: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/self-reference/

## 2. Newcomb's problem

**Statement.** A predictor with accuracy p puts $1,000,000 in an opaque box B iff it predicted you take only B; a transparent box A always holds $1,000. You choose either B alone (one-box) or both (two-box). Dominance: whatever is in B, taking both gives $1,000 more. Evidential expected value: one-boxing gets about p * 1,000,000, two-boxing about (1-p)*1,000,000 + 1,000, so one-boxing wins when p > 0.5005. The two principles disagree on the same numbers.

**Small formal model.** States S1 = "B full", S0 = "B empty"; acts O (one-box), T (two-box). Payoffs: O,S1 = 1,000,000; O,S0 = 0; T,S1 = 1,001,000; T,S0 = 1,000. Credences: P(S1|O) = p, P(S1|T) = 1-p, with p = 0.99 as base and p swept in [0.5, 1]. Causal credence: P(S1) = q fixed independent of act. Compute EDT vs CDT value and the crossover p* = 0.5005. Atoms: act, prediction, payoff; one dependency prediction -> payoff and one correlation prediction ~ act.

**Resolutions.**
- Evidential decision theory: one-box, since acts are evidence about the prediction.
- Causal decision theory: two-box, the box contents are causally fixed (Lewis, Joyce, Gibbard-Harper).
- Functional / updateless decision theories: choose as the output of the policy the predictor modelled, so one-box (Yudkowsky-Soares, Levinstein; Wolfgang Spohn's "dependency equilibria" also).
- Deny the stipulation / ratificationism: the case is incoherent or the agent should ratify (Jeffrey, Eells' tickle defence).

**Sources.**
- SEP, Causal Decision Theory: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/decision-causal/
- Nozick, "Newcomb's Problem and Two Principles of Choice" (1969), in *Essays in Honor of C. G. Hempel*.
- Ahmed, *Evidence, Decision and Causality* (CUP, 2014); SEP, Decision Theory: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/decision-theory/

## 3. Hempel's raven paradox (paradox of confirmation)

**Statement.** "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens". By Nicod's criterion (an instance of A and B confirms "all A are B") and the equivalence condition (evidence confirming a hypothesis confirms any equivalent one), a white shoe confirms "all ravens are black". Yet intuitively indoor ornithology via shoes is no evidence about ravens.

**Small formal model.** Finite universe of N objects, two Boolean features R (raven) and B (black); each object has four types RB, R~B, ~RB, ~R~B. Hypothesis H = no R~B objects. Concrete numbers: N = 100, prior over counts n(R) and n(~B) such as n(R)=5 or 10 and n(~B)=50 or 90 uniform, and sampling protocols (a) pick a random raven, (b) pick a random non-black object. Compute the Bayes factor P(E|H)/P(E|~H) for E = "this non-black object is a non-raven" under each protocol; it is >1 but tiny, and differs between protocols (Good's 1967 case shows a black raven can even disconfirm in a world sizing tweak).

**Resolutions.**
- Bayesian: accept the confirmation but it is negligible, since ravens are rare relative to non-black things (Hosiasson-Lindenbaum, Fitelson-Hawthorne under stated assumptions).
- Deny the equivalence condition or Nicod: confirmation is not preserved under logical equivalence in the naive way (Goodman-style projectibility; Quine on natural kinds).
- Sampling-protocol relativity: whether the observation confirms depends on how it was selected (Horwich; Good's counterexample).
- Deny the intuition: the white shoe does confirm and psychology misleads (Hempel's own bite-the-bullet).

**Sources.**
- SEP, Confirmation: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confirmation/
- Hempel, "Studies in the Logic of Confirmation", Mind 54 (1945); Good, "The White Shoe is a Red Herring", BJPS 17 (1967).
- Fitelson & Hawthorne, "How Bayesian Confirmation Theory Handles the Paradox of the Ravens" (2010), PhilPapers: https://philpapers.org/rec/FITHBC
